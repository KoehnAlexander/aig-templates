\section{Fundamentals of Basic Possibilistic Logic} \label{Fundamentals}
The following introduction on basic possibilistic logic is inspired by the presentation of Dubois and Prade \cite{dubois2014possibilistic}. This section gives a short overview of basic possibilistic logic. For more information, the work \cite{dubois2014possibilistic} is referred. \\
In this section we will first introduce main concepts of possibility theory. These concepts are necessary to understand possibilistic logic. Going further basic possibilistic logic is introduced with the syntax, axioms, inference rules and semantic aspects.
\subsection{Possibility Theory}
Possibility theory is explained in detail in \cite{dubois2023possibility}, \cite{zadeh1978}. Possibility theory relies on the concept of possibility distributions. 
\begin{definition}
	A possibility distribution is a function \( \pi \) that maps elements from a set of states \( U \) to a totally ordered scale \( S = \left[ 0 , 1 \right]  \), see \cite{dubois2014possibilistic}:
	\[
	\pi: U \to S.
	\]
\end{definition}
The value \( \pi(u) \) indicates the degree of possibility assigned to the state \( u \). This function represents the agent's knowledge and helps differentiate between states that are more or less plausible. A state \( u \) is impossible if \( \pi(u) = 0 \), whereas it is considered fully possible if \( \pi(u) = 1 \).
\begin{example}
	Consider a weather forecasting scenario where an agent assesses the possibility of different weather conditions for tomorrow. Let $ U = \{\text{sunny}, \text{cloudy}, \text{rainy}, \text{stormy}\} $ be the set of possible states. The agent assigns a possibility distribution $ \pi $ as follows: \\
	$ \pi(\text{sunny}) = 1, \quad \pi(\text{cloudy}) = 0.7, \quad \pi(\text{rainy}) = 0.3, \quad \pi(\text{stormy}) = 0$. \\
	Here, the agent is completely certain that a sunny day is possible ($\pi(\text{sunny}) = 1$), considers cloudy weather somewhat possible ($\pi(\text{cloudy}) = 0.7$), finds rain unlikely ($\pi(\text{rainy}) = 0.3$), and deems a storm impossible ($\pi(\text{stormy}) = 0$).
\end{example}

%\[

%\]
%An agent has complete knowledge if the possibility of one state is 1 and the possibility of all other states is 0. Complete ignorance is given it the possibility of all states is 1.

The basic measures of possibility theory model the possibility and necessity of events, see \cite{dubois2014possibilistic}. If the occurence of an event $ A $  is of interest possibility theory responds with both two measures calculated from the possibility distribution of $ A $. 
\begin{definition}
	The possibility of an event $ A $ is  $ \Pi(A) = \sup_{u \in A} \pi \left( u \right) $.
\end{definition}
The possibility is the maximum possibility of all the states of $ A $. The possibility evaluates the extent to which $ A $ is consistent with $ \pi $. Possibility measures satisfy the characteristic maxitivity property $ \Pi \left( A \cup B \right)  = \max \left( \Pi \left( A \right), \Pi \left(  B \right) \right) $. \\
\begin{definition}
	The necessity of an event $ A $  is $ N(A) = \inf_{u \notin A} \left( 1 - \pi \left( u \right) \right) $.
\end{definition}
The necessity is the minimum of the inversed possibility value of all states which are not in $ A $. The necessity evaluates the extent to which $ A $ is certainly implied by $ \pi $. Necessity measures satisfy the characteristic minimization property $ N \left( A \cap B \right)  = \min \left( N \left( A \right) , N \left( B \right) \right) $. The minimization property expresses that to be certain about $ A $ and $ B $, one has to be certain about $ A $ and to be certain about $ B $. \\
The duality of the two measures is expressed as $ N \left( A \right)  = 1 - \Pi \left( A^c \right) $, where $ A^c $ is the complement of $ A $.
\begin{example}
	Consider a possibility distribution $ \pi $ over a set of possible weather conditions: $ U = \{ \text{sunny}, \text{cloudy}, \text{rainy} \} $ with the values $ \pi(\text{sunny}) = 1 $, $ \pi(\text{cloudy}) = 0.7 $, and $ \pi(\text{rainy}) = 0.3 $. 
	
	The possibility of the event "not rainy" $ A = \{ \text{sunny}, \text{cloudy} \} $ is given by: 
	\[
	\Pi(A) = \sup_{u \in A} \pi(u) = \max(\pi(\text{sunny}), \pi(\text{cloudy})) = \max(1, 0.7) = 1.
	\]
	
	The necessity of "not rainy" is computed as:
	\[
	N(A) = \inf_{u \notin A} (1 - \pi(u)) = 1 - \pi(\text{rainy}) = 1 - 0.3 = 0.7.
	\]
	
	This means that the event "not rainy" is fully possible but not completely necessary.
\end{example}

\subsection{Formal foundations of Basic Possibilistic Logic}
In the following the foundations of basic possibilistic logic are layed out. First the syntax is introduced. Second the semantic aspects of the logic are derived and third the inference rules are explained. The last part covers the axioms of this logic.
\begin{definition}
	The syntax of a basic possibilistic logic formula is:
	\[
	(a, \alpha)
	\]
	where \(a\) is a classical logic formula (e.g., \(p \to q\)) and \(\alpha \) is the certainty level (minimum necessity degree of \( a \)). The classical logic formula can be from propositional logic or first-order logic, see \cite{dubois2014possibilistic}.
\end{definition}
The semantics of a possibilistic logic formula are based on the semantics of the classical logic formula. The pair $ \left( a , \alpha \right) $ is understood as $ N \left( a \right) > \alpha $. We can interpret that the formula $ a $ is at least certain to degree $ \alpha $. The higher $ \alpha $, the stronger the belief in $ a $. 
\begin{example}
	\((b \to f, 0.9)\) means that birds fly with certainty $ 0.9 $.
\end{example}
In the following inference rules of basic possibilistic logic will be explained which are needed later for the application of the logic. The certainty weakening rule states that if a formula is valid with a high certainty level, it is also valid with any lower certainty level.  If a formula holds with a certainty level $ \alpha $, it automatically implies that it holds with any lower level of certainty $ \beta $ because we are being less demanding, see equation \ref{Eq:Certainty_weakening}.
\begin{equation} \label{Eq:Certainty_weakening}
	\text{If } \beta \leq \alpha, \quad (a, \alpha) \vdash (a, \beta)
\end{equation}
The modus ponens rules states that if $ a $ to $ b $ is true with certainty level $ \alpha $, and $ a $ is also true with certainty level $ \alpha $ then $ b $ must be true with certainty level $ \alpha $, see equation \ref{Eq:Modus_ponens}.
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:Modus_ponens}
	(\neg a \lor b, \alpha), (a, \alpha) \vdash (b, \alpha), \quad \forall \alpha \in (0, 1]
\end{equation}
In classical logic, the resolution rule allows us to combine two clauses that share a literal to eliminate that literal and infer a new clause. In possibilistic logic, the resolution rule preserves the certainty level $ \alpha $, because the inferred formula $ b \lor c $ cannot be more certain than the least certain premise, see equation \ref{Eq:Resolution}.
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:Resolution}
	(\neg a \lor b, \alpha), \quad (a \lor c, \alpha) \vdash (b \lor c, \alpha)
\end{equation}
The weakest link resolution rule extends the resolution rule by the concept of different certainty levels $ \alpha $ and $ \beta $. The weakest link resolution reflects the idea that the certainty of the conclusion cannot exceed the weakest certainty of the premises. If one of the premises is less certain, it becomes the bottleneck for the certainty of the conclusion, see equation \ref{Eq:Weakest_link_resolution}.
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:Weakest_link_resolution}
	(\neg a \lor b, \alpha), \quad (a \lor c, \beta) \vdash (b \lor c, \min(\alpha, \beta))
\end{equation}
\begin{definition}
	A possibilistic knowledge base is as a collection of possibilistic formulas:
	\begin{equation}\label{Eq:Knowledge_base}
		\Gamma = \{(a_i, \alpha_i), i = 1, \dots, m\}
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}
\begin{example} \label{example:medical}
	Consider a medical diagnosis system where symptoms are associated with certain diseases with varying degrees of certainty. The possibilistic knowledge base would be:
	\[
	\Gamma = \{ (	\text{Fever} \Rightarrow \text{Flu}, 0.9), (	\text{Cough} \Rightarrow \text{Cold}, 0.7), (	\text{Headache} \Rightarrow \text{Migraine}, 0.8) \}.
	\]
	This means that having a fever strongly suggests flu with a certainty level of 0.9, having a cough suggests a cold with a certainty level of 0.7, and having a headache suggests a migraine with a certainty level of 0.8. These possibilistic rules help in reasoning under uncertainty in medical diagnosis.
\end{example}
\begin{definition}
	The $ \alpha $-cut of knowledge base is defined as:
	\begin{equation}\label{Eq:Knowledge_base_cut}
		\Gamma_\alpha = \{(a_i, \alpha_i) \in \Gamma \mid \alpha_i \geq \alpha\}
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}
The $ \alpha $-cut of knowledge base only contains those formulas from the origin knowledge base with a certainty level of at least $ \alpha $. It has a nested structure where the $ \alpha $ knwoledge base is a part of the $ \beta $ knowledge base if $ \alpha $ is higher than $ \beta $.
\begin{example}
	Proceeding with example \ref{example:medical}, the $ \alpha $-cut of knowledge base with $ \alpha $ equals 0.8 is:
	\[
	\Gamma_\alpha = \{ (	\text{Fever} \Rightarrow \text{Flu}, 0.9), (	\text{Headache} \Rightarrow \text{Migraine}, 0.8) \}.
	\]
\end{example}

A possibilistic inference \((a, \alpha)\) depends only on formulas with certainty levels \(\geq \alpha\), see equation \ref{Eq:Inference}:
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:Inference}
	\Gamma \vdash (a, \alpha) \iff \Gamma_\alpha \vdash (a, \alpha)
\end{equation}
$ \Gamma \vdash (a, \alpha) $ means that $ (a, \alpha) $ can be derived from $ \Gamma $. If we want to infer from the knowledge base, we can only look on the formulas with a certainty of at least $ \alpha $. This means that a inference is only valid if it is valid with the $ \alpha $-cut of a knowledge base. \\
An important attribute of knowledge bases is the inconsistency level. 
\begin{definition}
	The inconsistency level is defined as:
	\begin{equation}\label{Eq:Inconsistency_level}
		\text{inc-l}(\Gamma) = \max\{\alpha \mid \Gamma \vdash (\bot, \alpha)\}
	\end{equation}
	, where $ \bot $ represents an inconsistent state in logic.
\end{definition}
It is the highest certainty level at which the knowledge base becomes inconsistent. The properties of the inconsistency level show that all formulas which have a higher certainty level than the inconsistency level are safe form inconsistency, see equation \ref{Eq:Inconsistency_level}.

\section{Analysis and Development of Examples} \label{Examples}

\subsection{The Penguin Problem}
One of the classical examples in non-monotonic reasoning is the penguin problem. Basic possibilistic logic provides a structured way to handle exceptions of general rules like "birds fly" by associating rules with certainty levels and defining a preference ordering over possible worlds.
\subsubsection{Default Rules}
The knowledge base of this example consists of three default rules that represent generic knowledge about birds and penguins:
\begin{align*}
	d_1 &: b \rightarrow f \quad (\text{"Birds fly"}), \\
	d_2 &: p \rightarrow \neg f \quad (\text{"Penguins do not fly"}), \\
	d_3 &: p \rightarrow b \quad (\text{"Penguins are birds"}).
\end{align*}
In the following the goal is to assign certainty levels to these rules, to get a possibilistic knowledge base. In order to find this knowledge base, the following steps are necessary. First the constraints from the rules are constructed. Second, the possible worlds are ranked by the constraints. With the ranked worlds the certainty of the rules is calculated.
\subsubsection{Constraints}
From the default rules, a set of comparative possibility constraints can be constructed:
\begin{align*}
	C_1 &: b \land f >_\Pi b \land \neg f \quad \text{(birds fly)}, \\
	C_2 &: p \land \neg f >_\Pi p \land f \quad \text{(penguins do not fly)}, \\
	C_3 &: p \land b >_\Pi p \land \neg b \quad \text{(penguins are birds)}.
\end{align*}
For example, $C_1$ states that the worlds where birds fly are more possible than worlds where birds do not fly. These constraints establish an ordering over possible worlds, which will be illuminated next. 
\subsubsection{Possible Worlds}

\begin{align*}
	\Omega = \{ \omega_0, \omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3, \omega_4, \omega_5, \omega_6, \omega_7 \}.
\end{align*}
Based on the constraints, these worlds are ranked to reflect which interpretations are more plausible. \\

In table \ref{tab:worlds_rules} each line represents a world with the combination of the three predicates and the satisfaction or violation of a default rule. In this table it can be observed that no world satisfies all rules, which highlight the core problem. The worlds \( \omega_0 \) and \( \omega_2 \) do not satisfy or violate any rule. \\
The first default rule, \( d_1: b \rightarrow f \) ("Birds fly"), increases the plausibility of worlds where birds fly (\( b \land f \)) compared to those where birds do not fly (\( b \land \neg f \)). This rule affects the ranking between worlds \( \omega_6 \), \( \omega_7 \) (where the bird flies) and \( \omega_4, \omega_5 \) (where the bird does not fly). \\
The second default rule, \( d_2: p \rightarrow \neg f \) ("Penguins do not fly"), prioritizes worlds where penguins do not fly (\( p \land \neg f \)) over those where penguins fly (\( p \land f \)). This affects the relative plausibility of \( \omega_5 \), \( \omega_1 \) (where the penguin does not fly) compared to \( \omega_7 \) and \( \omega_3 \) (where the penguin flies). \\
The third default rule, \( d_3: p \rightarrow b \) ("Penguins are birds"), ranks the worlds \( \omega_5 \), \( \omega_7 \) higher than \( \omega_1 \), \( \omega_3 \). \\
We can insert the worlds in which the rule holds into the constraints, as these worlds represent the rule:
\begin{align*}
	C_1 &: \max(\pi(\omega_6), \pi(\omega_7)) > \max(\pi(\omega_4), \pi(\omega_5)), \\
	C_2 &: \max(\pi(\omega_5), \pi(\omega_1)) > \max(\pi(\omega_3), \pi(\omega_7)), \\
	C_3 &: \max(\pi(\omega_5), \pi(\omega_7)) > \max(\pi(\omega_1), \pi(\omega_3)).
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Creating Knowledge Base}
We calculate the necessity degrees from the possibility of the worlds as follows with the duality relationship of possibility and necessity:
\begin{align*}
	N(\neg p \lor \neg f) &= \min \{ 1 - \pi(\omega) \mid \omega \models p \land f \} \\
	&= \min(1 - \pi(\omega_3), 1 - \pi(\omega_7)) = \frac{2}{3} \\
	N(\neg b \lor f) &= \min \{ 1 - \pi(\omega) \mid \omega \models b \land \neg f \} \\
	&= \min(1 - \pi(\omega_4), 1 - \pi(\omega_5)) = \frac{1}{3} \\
	N(\neg p \lor b) &= \min \{ 1 - \pi(\omega) \mid \omega \models p \land \neg b\} \\
	&= \min(1 - \pi(\omega_1), 1 - \pi(\omega_3)) = \frac{2}{3}
\end{align*}
Thus, we obtain the possibilistic knowledge base:
\[
K = \{ (\neg p \lor \neg f, \frac{2}{3}), (\neg p \lor b, \frac{2}{3}), (\neg b \lor f, \frac{1}{3}) \}.
\]
The possibilitic knowledge base is visualized in figure \ref{fig:rules_ranking}.

\subsubsection{Reasoning with Uncertain Information}
In this part we can find out if Tweety flies by introducing information about Tweety. First we introduce that Tweety is a bird:
\begin{align*}
	F = \{ (b, 1) \} \quad \text{(Tweety is a bird with certainty 1)}.
\end{align*}
From the rule $(\neg b \lor f, 1/3)$ in the knowledge base $K$, we can infer:
\begin{align*}
	(\neg b \lor f, 1/3) \quad \text{and} \quad (b, 1) \vdash (f, \min(1, 1/3)) = (f, 1/3).
\end{align*}
Thus, we conclude that Tweety flies with a certainty level of $\frac{1}{3}$. We can see the result graphically in figure \ref{fig:firstinformation}. The violet arrow symbolizes the inference with the weakest link resolution rule. \\

If new information states that Tweety is also a penguin:
\begin{align*}
	F = \{ (b,1), (p,1) \} \quad \text{(Tweety is a penguin with certainty 1)},
\end{align*}
then, using $(\neg p \lor \neg f, 2/3)$, we derive:
\begin{align*}
	(\neg p \lor \neg f, 2/3) \quad \text{and} \quad (p, 1) \vdash (\neg f, \min(1, 2/3)) = (\neg f, 2/3).
\end{align*}
Since we now have conflicting conclusions:
\begin{align*}
	(\neg f, 2/3) \quad \text{and} \quad (f, 1/3) \vdash (\bot, \min(2/3, 1/3)) = (\bot, 1/3),
\end{align*}
the system detects an inconsistency of level $\frac{1}{3}$. Given the principle that higher certainty information takes advantage, the inference that Tweety flies is drowned, leading to the final conclusion:
\begin{align*}
	K \cup F \vdash (\neg f, 2/3),
\end{align*}
which means \textit{"Tweety does not fly"} with a certainty of $\frac{2}{3}$. \\
